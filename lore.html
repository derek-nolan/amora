Gods, forces, and recurring structures

Lore

Amora's central conflict isn't good against evil — it's the struggle over whether safety, love, law, memory, and order remain chosen relationships or become systems that command people without meaningful exit. These are the recurring concepts that struggle plays out through, across both timelines.

Amora and Vanmere

Two names that sit at different scales and are easy to conflate. Amora is the world itself — the setting as a whole, the name of the campaign, and the name this site uses for the whole continuity project across both timelines. Vanmere is the continent where nearly everything on this site takes place — Highever, Evermere, Durrel Ridge, the Badlands, Brevair, Rimeholt, and every other region on the Atlas sit on Vanmere. The maps in the Atlas's interactive viewer are all maps of Vanmere specifically, labelled by name in their own legends. Campaign B16 establishes the first confirmed geography beyond Vanmere's own shores: Nareth Isle, a large inhabited island west-southwest across open water. Its absence from earlier maps reflects their own coastal focus, not late discovery — sailors could always reach it, the maps simply hadn't extended that far. Beyond Nareth, what else exists on Amora remains genuinely open rather than invented.

Deep History: Before the Heartwardens

Nearly a millennium before Zethyr and Taren revive the Heartwardens in Campaign I, two catastrophes reshaped Vanmere and left marks every later age still lives inside — a shattered paladin tradition, and a desert where a magical war once was. The three entries below trace that history in order.

The Oath Wars

Later surviving traditions explain the Oath Wars through the opposed legacies of Aureth and Velis, remembered as a single sacred line that came apart over what an oath was actually meant to protect. Aureth's legacy is associated with illumination, individual conscience, and mercy — that an oath could never excuse cruelty, and that questioning an unjust command was a paladin's responsibility, not a betrayal of it. Velis's legacy is associated with hierarchy, restraint, and disciplined obedience — that personal conscience could not be allowed to undermine order. Whether their separation literally triggered the war, represents a later historical simplification of a more complex fracture, or was one strand among several remains unresolved; B18's own ground-level view of the war shows Crown, Sanctuary, Liberty, and the traditions of the Seven Refusal as distinct positions in their own right, not simple proxies for one side or the other. Neither legacy is remembered as simply villainous — both are said to have genuinely believed the other doctrine would destroy the world if it won.

The wars that followed weren't two clean armies. Individual orders, knightly houses, and bastions split unevenly along the fault line — some backed one tradition outright, some improvised compromise oaths, others fought mainly to protect their own land. Entire houses divided; members of the same family fought under opposing vows for decades. The old unified tradition never recovered. Later ages remember militant Oath Remnants, each claiming descent from a different surviving vow, and the much later Order of the Reforged Light exists specifically to restore the unity the Oath Wars destroyed.

Dated at last (Campaign B17). The main period of the Oath Wars runs roughly c. -960 to c. -914 ARV — see the Ancient Chronology for the full dated sequence, shared pre-history for both timelines.

The war on the ground (Campaign B18). B18 is the first campaign to actually play through the Oath Wars rather than excavate its aftermath — roughly twenty-two years in, at c. -938 ARV, inside the fertile pre-Shattering city of Alderwick. It shows the conflict as lived pressure rather than one clean battle line: Crown, Sanctuary, and Liberty personnel dispute jurisdiction over a disputed grain convoy and a contested levy at Westfield, while a schismatic movement calling itself First Crown — led by Edmund Shaw, a genuine Crown commander who genuinely believes fragmented authority is what's actually prolonging the war — runs a compromised clandestine network beneath Crown Hall's own roof. B18 is explicit that Shaw is only one figure in a much larger conflict; it deliberately doesn't universalise Alderwick's local schism into the whole war, and leaves Shaw's own reckoning unresolved for later campaigns to pick up. See Campaign B18 for the full account, and Hugh Bellamy and Adam Kersey for its central pair.

The clearest single documented moment from the war is the Seven Refusal, c. -919 ARV: seven oath traditions — Redemption, Crown, Hearth, Devotion, Sanctuary, Liberty, and Fellowship — refused a "righteous slaughter" ordered in Velis's own name, each finding that none of their actual sworn oaths required what their masters were demanding. A Fellowship paladin, Ser Auren Valeth, carried that collective refusal to his own grave rather than raise his sword against people he'd already held a wall beside. B17 doesn't inflate this into the whole war — it's one documented moment where oath-bound paladins refused to let hierarchy override the vows they'd actually sworn, recovered from a crypt at the Western Oath-Fort.

Velis did not command this. A hidden inscription at the same crypt adds one of B17's most important qualifications: "Velis did not command this. Men spoke in his name. We obeyed them until obedience became the sin." The atrocities carried out in Velis's tradition weren't necessarily his own will — a distinction the site preserves rather than collapsing his doctrine into simple villainy.

Tarenus Durrel, later the first Warden of Heart, was already a Devotion paladin during this era and insisted Heart be kept out of "the quarrel of the sworn" — the oaths were their bearers' own, and Heart must never be made a weapon between them. B17 doesn't invent a specific battle for him; it places him securely in this world and shows the principle he held, leaving his exact combat role unresolved. See Tarenus Durrel.

A continuity lock. The Oath of the Veil — the oath Taren creates when he turns the Heartwardens into the Veilwardens during the Dominion — is not the same thing as Velis's ancient tradition, even though the two rhyme thematically. Taren may have borrowed, rediscovered, or simply echoed older authoritarian oath ideas; any Oath of the Veil zealots found among later Oath Remnants are later arrivals to the region, not survivors of the original war. Whether Aureth and Velis are the same figures as Luminara and Shah under older names, direct predecessors, or something else entirely is a genuine open gap in the record — not something this site should silently resolve by assuming they're identical.

Open gaps: the individual Oath Wars factions beyond the seven refusing traditions, their leaders and major battles, and the precise relationship between Aureth/Velis and Luminara/Shah all remain unresolved in the source record.

The War of Ash and Glass, and the Shattering

In the disorder surrounding the Oath Wars, a second and very different conflict broke out — this one over arcane power rather than doctrine. Sorcerous bloodlines, drawing on inherited power and direct access to the world's deeper magic, fought warlock covenants, whose strength came instead from pacts, bound entities, and forbidden knowledge anchored through agreement rather than blood. What began the dispute isn't recorded; whether it grew out of the Oath Wars' chaos or ran alongside it as its own struggle over magical authority remains unclear. What's certain is that it escalated far past any single incident.

The Shattering. The war ended when both sides reached further than the land could hold. The sorcerous houses drew directly on Vanmere's foundational ley structure; the warlock covenants tried to anchor vast, interconnected pacts into that same network at the same time. The two systems collided. Ley-lines ruptured out of alignment, settlements were destroyed outright, stone and soil burned or turned to glass, magical weather became self-sustaining, and entire sorcerous houses and warlock covenants were wiped out along with whatever they'd been fighting to control. Neither side won — the infrastructure they wanted was ruined along with them. The Shattering does not only create the Badlands; it also breaks the older southeastern land connection shown on Campaign B18's own map (c. -938 ARV), severing what had been a broad, inhabited land bridge to the landmass later known as Brevair and beginning its later geographic isolation and arid transformation. See the Atlas for the ancient geography in full.

Campaign B16 provides the first direct archaeological evidence of the war encountered in modern Timeline B: an ancient transit chamber beneath Rimeholt contains material dating to the War of Ash and Glass period or very close to it, including damaged imagery associated with both sorcerous and warlock traditions, and a fragmentary warning reading "do not permit the bound... to choose the passage." This dates activity at the site and physically ties it to the same catastrophe already established as the Badlands' origin — it does not identify who actually built the chamber, what the unidentified black-gold phenomenon reaching through it is, or the war's original trigger. What's newly known and what remains genuinely unknown are kept separate rather than let one imply the other.

The Great Seals were set during this war (Campaign B17). The war runs roughly c. -932 to c. -905 ARV; its closing years overlap directly with the Oath Wars' own end — glass driven through Ser Auren Valeth's armour at the Western Oath-Fort physically confirms the two conflicts' human consequences met at the same sites, without collapsing into one war. Crucially, this is also when the permanent Great Seals were set: Frost, Stone, and Flame within their own measure, Life, Light, and Shadow to their Houses' limits, Storm last, and Heart between all seven only once they held, c. -913 ARV. The Great Seals therefore existed for roughly eight years before the Shattering ended the war — the Shattering didn't create the Seal system, it struck one that had only just finished forming. See the Ancient Chronology for the full dated sequence and the Magic Codex for the recovered Binder/House/Setting terminology this discovery establishes.

Open gap: the exact trigger of the War of Ash and Glass remains unrecorded.

The Badlands' True Origin

The Shattering is the buried truth behind the region every campaign page since Campaign I has simply called the Badlands. Now dated to c. -905 ARV (Campaign B17) — see the Ancient Chronology. Popular memory reduced the whole war to a single rumour — that "mages conducted a catastrophic experiment centuries ago" — which is also why mages became objects of general suspicion across Vanmere. The Badlands already exist by 0 ARV, proving neither the Dominion nor any later calamity created them; they're a distinct interior region of Vanmere, separate from Menge, matching the Shattering's consequences closely: wild-magic storms, violently shifted stone, shattered towers, buried vaults, artefacts "too powerful to exist," and expeditions few people return from — quietly sponsored by nobles and mages even though the region is officially shunned.

Storm did not cause this. The Storm Great Seal was successfully set before the Shattering, alongside the other seven — Campaign B19 confirms this directly, through Storm's own preserved memory. The self-sustaining magical weather that defines the Badlands is a consequence of the Shattering itself, not of Storm failing or unleashing anything; for more than twelve centuries, Storm has been containing that wound rather than causing it. ("The Badlands are the wound; Storm is the hand pressed against it" is the understanding Benjamin Strake reaches in B19 while touched by that memory — his own interpretation of what he's shown, not a line recovered from any ancient record.) A major pressure release during B19 substantially calms the Badlands' extreme storm regime for the first time in over twelve centuries, opening large areas to renewed exploration — but it doesn't heal the Shattering's physical damage: the glassed terrain, shattered towers, and altered creatures remain exactly as dangerous as before. See Campaign B19 and the Magic Codex for the full account.

Later events damaged the Badlands further without creating them. Taren's Dominion used parts of the region for exile and penal settlements, mined Veilstone there, and diverted ancient ley-rivers to feed imperial infrastructure. Centuries later, the Sundering Tempest's aftershocks fractured the region further into glass canyons, which nomads, scavengers, and relic hunters now occupy. The clean order is: War of Ash and Glass → the Shattering → the original Badlands, followed by Dominion exploitation, and later the Sundering Tempest's additional damage. Campaign B19 is the first of these later events to substantially improve rather than worsen conditions there — calming the Shattering's magical weather without healing the Shattering itself.

A separate naming issue. One older Campaign IV-era source refers to a "Badlands of Menge," supposedly torn open when Light and Veil energies collided during a Light uprising against Taren, and describes frozen auroras and crystal spires. That can't be the same place as the primary Badlands — the real Badlands predate Taren entirely, the Atlas keeps Menge and the Badlands as separate regions throughout, and the Shattering already provides an earlier, better-documented origin. The most likely explanation is that the older summary used "Badlands" as a generic label for a different, later-blighted part of Menge — a scar that probably deserves its own name (the Menge Rift, the Aurora Wastes, the Blighted North) rather than being confused with the original Badlands.

The Dominion

Referenced constantly across Timeline A — the Age of Dominion, Dominion infrastructure, Dominion-restoration remnants — without ever getting its own explanation on this site until now. The Dominion is Taren Durrel's empire, founded in Campaign II after he assassinates Brevair's emperor and reshapes the Heartwardens into the Veilwardens. Its structure is deceptively simple: one empire, one god-emperor, one consort — Taren at the apex, Zethyr bound to him through the Chain as living proof and power source at once. Greymount becomes Greymount Sanctum, the Dominion's capital; Evermere shifts from sanctuary to imperial pleasure-and-law administration. The Dominion achieves genuine, terrifying stability — it isn't a fragile tyranny that collapses under its own weight, it's a working system that ends only because Zethyr chooses to end it, severing the Veil Core at the cost of his own life.

The Dominion's afterlife matters as much as its reign. It doesn't simply vanish with Taren's death — its logic keeps recurring across Timeline A in new forms: Cael's Echo Chain rebuilding attempt in Campaign III, Coren Veyne's reborn Confluence architecture in Campaign IV, and the Ley-Heir structures that keep re-emerging whenever a bonded pair's devotion gets redirected toward permanent control rather than mutual choice. See Ley-Heirs and Severance for the two concepts that directly answer it — one names the recurring inheritance structure the Dominion's logic keeps rebuilding itself into; the other names the deliberate act of refusing to let it.

The Durrel Pattern

Timeline A begins with the bond between Taren and Zethyr Durrel — love, psychic connection, and shared service that becomes the template for a recurring shape: a commanding or structuring figure paired with a devoted, anchoring counterpart. Every later age inherits, reconstructs, ritualises, codifies, or consciously rejects this pattern in some form. Timeline B prevents the original Dominion by unmaking Taren and Zethyr entirely, but doesn't eliminate the world's tendency to build succession systems around powerful dyads — see Ley-Heirs below.

What actually turns devotion dangerous. Devotion itself is never the corruption in these stories — it's one of the setting's most genuinely positive forces, and it's Luminara's own domain. What changes a commanding-figure-plus-devoted-anchor pair from a healthy bond into something that conquers cities is specifically whether that devotion gets redirected toward Shah's model instead of Luminara's: worship in place of witness, permanence in place of consent, a person's reverence treated as a resource to be drawn on rather than a gift freely given. Cameron's rise in Campaign B11 is the clearest example on this site — his own doctrine states it outright: reverence directed at him isn't a substitute for reaching Shah, it's part of how Shah is reached. Gavren's devotion in that campaign is real and freely chosen throughout; what makes it a tragedy is that Cameron treats it as raw ritual material rather than the actual person offering it. The same shape — real feeling, weaponised through worship rather than overridden by force — recurs across several of Timeline B's later campaigns: B10's Rhalen Valecour building an entire civic-control network on Delryn's own devotion; B13's Thorn House operating on an even sharper variant — its own doctrine holds that no one who conditions another is safe from being conditioned themselves by someone more senior in the same structure, proven when Rul Hask, Caelen's original conditioner, is later remade by Sareth using the exact methods he'd used first; and B16's Jarek Flint, whose hidden conditioning of Nick works precisely because it reinforces feelings that were already genuinely there, rather than manufacturing them outright. Where a working genuinely does override consent instead of merely exploiting it — as opposed to feeding on freely-given devotion — that distinction is called out explicitly on the campaign's own page and in the Magic Codex.

The Name Taren in House Durrel

Taren is an old repeating name within House Durrel, carried as an act of remembrance rather than prophecy.

The Durrels have a long tradition of giving certain descendants the name Taren. By Timeline B, the practice is ancient enough that nobody can point to a single generation in which it formally began. It sits alongside the House's memory of Tarenus Durrel, the ancient founding Heartbinder from whom the recorded Warden tradition descends — first visited directly in Campaign B7, where this naming tradition itself is first established. Tarenus himself remains a fixed ancestral figure at Durrel Ridge, although the generations between him and the later family are deliberately incomplete.

To the Durrels, naming a child Taren is a vow of remembrance. It expresses continuity with the House, its Warden duty, and the generations that came before. It does not mean that the child is believed to be a reincarnation, chosen one, destined ruler, or return of a particular ancestor.

That distinction matters enormously in Timeline B. The Taren Durrel who would have become the Veiled Emperor in Timeline A was unmade in 0 ARV, before that future could occur. Yet his name did not disappear from House Durrel. The family continued using it because the name belonged to the House before it belonged to any one infamous bearer.

Over the generations, this created a complicated cultural weight around the name. A Taren might be regarded with particular interest by older members of the family, especially those concerned with the Heartwarden inheritance, but the name carries expectation rather than supernatural authority. After one later bearer of the name was murdered at Highever, Durrel tradition recorded that the House would "watch for the next bearer." That didn't mean waiting for a foretold saviour — it meant the name itself had become part of the family's rhythm: eventually another child would be entrusted with it, and with the memory it represented.

The Taren Durrel born around 116 ARV, who eventually becomes Warden of Durrel Ridge in Campaign B9, is therefore not called Taren because anyone knew what he would become. He is one more bearer of an ancestral family name. His significance comes from what he chooses to do with it.

House Durrel repeats the name Taren as remembrance, never as prophecy. A Taren inherits a name and a history — not a destiny.

The Western Icewardens

A third branch of the Frost lineage, distinct from both the fallen Icebinders and the surviving northern Frostborn — the clearest world-historical proof that a Seal lineage's fallen branch and its legitimate custodians can diverge into more than two outcomes. Roughly nine centuries before Campaign B16 rediscovers them, the Shattering damaged reporting and access across the newly-set Great Seal network. Rather than risk memory failing in a single place, the surviving Frost tradition deliberately split: northern households stayed with the Seal itself, while three households travelled west by agreement, carrying a duplicated "second measure," verification standards, and historical record — explicitly renouncing any claim to the northern Frost Seal in the process. That settlement forgot why it had been founded and became the ordinary town of Rimeholt.

The Western branch is organised around two paired offices unique to this specific tradition — the Custodian, who preserves, verifies, remembers, and braces; and the Way-hand, who opens, releases, moves, and returns. Neither commands the other, and the pairing shouldn't be assumed onto any other Seal lineage without evidence. Recognition determines who holds either office — not bloodline proximity: Maraine Soren carries the blood and was deliberately never presented, a legitimate choice rather than a failure to inherit, while her son Nicholas Soren is formally recognised as Custodian in Campaign B16 and confirms the branch's actual founding purpose in B17: not exile, not apostasy, but a deliberate redundancy system so that if the north's own custody ever fails, Frost still has a trustworthy baseline against which the world can be measured. See the Magic Codex for the full mechanical detail, and the Ancient Chronology for exactly when the split occurred.

Luminara

The Radiant One — goddess of justice, mercy, and light. Highever's early paladins, the Silver Dawn, and later Light-resistance traditions in Timeline B all look to her as the model for compassionate order; Lio Serrin begins as her squire before the founding rupture makes him Timeline B's first Anchor, and Taren Durrel-Ridge later requests formal blessing under her name to publicly align Durrel Ridge with the Light in Campaign B9.

Practically, what recognition under Luminara actually tracks is genuine chosen alignment, not institutional status or purity. Silver Bastion's own Saint-Wards, altered under occupation to reject or burn visible oath-light, still recognise Mike Rookvale — an unsworn rogue with no formal religious standing — the instant he commits to carrying one, naming him by identity and choice rather than office (Campaign B14). The ward's condition was never ordination; it was simply that the working couldn't claim, bind, convert, or oath-lock him against his will. This is the direct opposite of Shah's mechanism below: where Shah-aligned power treats someone's genuine devotion as fuel for another's control, Luminara's recognition depends on the choice remaining the bearer's own all the way through — see The Durrel Pattern for how the two consistently get set against each other across both timelines.

Shah / the Veil

The shadowed divine or metaphysical axis associated in Timeline B with sovereignty, hierarchy, and consolidation. The sources use "Veil" for several related things — a magical force, an ideology, an entity, and a category of resonance — so context matters when reading a campaign page. In Timeline A, "the Veil" more often refers specifically to the reality-shaping magic Taren draws on to found the Dominion.

Practically, Shah-aligned power draws on ritual reverence directed at a person rather than at Luminara — small, personal-scale devotional practice rather than any state-spanning apparatus. This is the actual mechanism behind the site's recurring "commanding figure, devoted anchor" pairs when they tip toward harm: not a spell that overrides someone's will, but an ideology and a set of ritual practices that treat a person's genuine devotion as fuel. See The Durrel Pattern above.

The First Authority of Alderwick

Ancient civic infrastructure predating the Great Seals entirely — recovered in Campaign B18, roughly twenty-five years before the Seals are ever set. The First Authority was an emergency public-works office for "waters, crossings and common works," not an ancient monarchy and not a permanent supreme government. Its own founding articles state the constitutional principle plainly: the Authority commands the reserve only until ordinary civic administration is restored, and no emergency power survives the emergency that created it. Public necessity never became martial ownership — keepers were selected for competence across humans, dwarves, and elves alike: engineers, surveyors, magistrates, watermasters, and stonewrights, not a single ruling house.

Not a Seal system. A recurring administrative mark — three concentric squares pierced by a vertical line — links old Alderwick water and crossing sites, and is confirmed First Authority civic marking, explicitly not a Great Seal symbol. A second, deeper "crossed-circle" reserve layer exists beneath the ordinary First Authority network — real, limited to only four known nodes, and genuinely unresolved; the First Authority may have inherited this deeper layer rather than built it. This site does not merge the First Authority, its crossed-circle reserve, the later Rimeholt transit works, or B17's eight-around-centre Seal apparatus into one ancient civilisation. Similar age or fitted-stone construction is not evidence of one shared origin — see the Magic Codex for the Great Seals' own separate, dated history.

The Great Seals and Warden Lineages

The oldest magic system in Amora, predating both the Dominion and the Ley-Heir cycle — a distributed custodial network, not a ladder with Heart at the top. Every Great Seal has its own Warden lineage, trained to recognise and stabilise the specific pressure it regulates; Heart moderates the wider network rather than ruling it. "Binder" — Heartbinder, Flamebinder, Icebinder — is the older, incomplete bloodline-affinity term; "Warden" names the fuller custodial duty and institution those bloodlines actually carried. See the Magic Codex for the full registry, locations, and confidence levels; see Factions for the lineages as living institutions.

Campaign I is substantially about reconnecting and restoring these fragmented custodial traditions, not simply visiting elemental batteries: the Heart Seal — the founding metaphysical structure beneath Silver Bastion — is discovered by Zethyr and Taren, who then go on to stabilise several further Seals in turn. The northern Frost tradition they encounter is already in crisis by this point: the legitimate Icewarden lineage has fractured, and a fallen faction remembered as the Icebinders is spreading destructive frost through Menge. This distinction matters — Icewardens were the Seal's genuine original custodians; Icebinders are specifically their fallen branch, not a neutral name for the whole Frost lineage.

Campaign B16, roughly 340 years later, adds a third branch to that same Frost lineage — the Western Icewardens, who split off generations before the northern fall, carrying no portion of the Frost Seal and making no claim to the northern sanctuary. Their whole tradition became preservation rather than power, founding what became the ordinary town of Rimeholt and surviving precisely by ceasing to look like a Warden settlement. B16 also confirms the current working registry of eight Great Seals — Heart, Flame, Stone, Storm, Life, Shadow, Light, and Frost — superseding an earlier list that had included Aether, Veil, and Balance; see the Magic Codex for the full registry and the older terminology's current unresolved status. The clearest single principle underlying all of it: a Warden lineage's blood preserves the possibility of office; neither blood nor ancestry automatically confers authority, and a fallen branch doesn't morally redefine the force it once guarded.

Timeline B's Campaign B2 complicates this further in a genuinely important way: the Frostborn, discovered at Hollowrest, preserve the unfallen Icewarden inheritance, proving the northern tradition never fully collapsed the way Campaign I's own crisis suggested. But the Order of Anchors' conclusion at the time — that the Icebinders were "never a cult" — reads, in fuller context, as a sincere but incomplete historical overcorrection: they'd found legitimate survivors and mistakenly generalised that finding to the fallen faction as a whole. Both things are true at once — the Icewarden line survived more intact than anyone realised, and Campaign I's original Icebinder threat was genuine. See Canon Ledger for how this site handles the overcorrection explicitly.

Severance

The recurring act of deliberately breaking a Chain, bond, or cycle rather than letting it calcify into rule. First enacted by Zethyr at the fall of the Dominion in Campaign II, though historians of the time have no word for what he's done yet. Edrin Durrel is the one who names it, understands it, and consciously repeats it in Campaign III — turning a desperate act into a deliberate philosophy. Talen Marric later speaks it as final doctrine in Campaign XXI: "No chains, no heirs, only choice." Severance is Amora's answer to Dominion logic — proof an imperfect world with choice beats a perfect one without it.

Not the same thing as a magical severance. Timeline B also uses "severance" for a specific technical procedure — cutting a foreign magical attachment away from someone's own power, as Elara Soren performs on Deren Flint and later on Jarek Flint's foreign black-gold claim (Campaigns B16–B17). That's a rescue, not a philosophy — removing something parasitic rather than breaking a bond that would otherwise calcify into rule. The word is shared; the concept isn't. See the Magic Codex for the Western Icewarden mechanics behind the technical version.

Ley-Heirs

A metaphysical inheritance structure created by the Dominion to preserve its will through succession. Thought destroyed after the Severing, but re-established through Clause XI in Campaign XI. In Timeline B, Caen and Vareth (Lio) develop their own Ley-Heir architecture during Campaign B5 — the Sovereign-Reliquary structure — and Rhalen Valecour's Keystone in B10 is a civic-scale variant of the same underlying logic, routing an entire nation's authority through a single living conduit rather than a whole dyad. All three confirm the same lesson: preventing the original Dominion doesn't prevent new succession and control systems from re-forming around a bonded pair or a single vulnerable person — see The Dominion for the pattern they all answer to.

Resonance, Anchors & Dyads

Timeline B and 4CE-era Timeline A both develop a magic system built on bonded pairs rather than artefacts: an Anchor (stabilising figure) and a Thread or Voice (the paired counterpart), whose trust and consent generate magical effect. First formalised by the Order of Anchors in Timeline B's Campaign B1, and independently rediscovered in Timeline A's Campaign XXIV as "Living Dyads" — proof, in both timelines, that the old Taren/Zethyr pattern can be inherited without its abuse of control.

Dating: ARV and the Four Cyberpunk Eras

ARV is the single continuous calendar across both timelines (see the Chronology page for exact dates). "CE" doesn't mean Common Era — it labels the points where Amora's history swings into a cyberpunk state rather than climbing toward technology in a straight line. Only the Third and Fourth eras are named directly in the source material; the First and Second are reconstructed here from the text's own clues (Campaign XII's note that the world rebuilds "after machine/cyber ages," plural, and Campaign XV's reference to "the Cataclysm" that precedes it).

First Cyberpunk Era — Campaigns IX–XI (Age of Machines through Age of Clause), reverting to medieval at Campaign XII.
Second Cyberpunk Era — Campaigns XIII–XIV, ending in the Cataclysm that closes the Dominion Cycle.
Third Cyberpunk Age — Campaigns XV–XVI (Neon Concord, the Corridor Crown), named directly in the source; collapses into the Age of Disconnection at the start of Campaign XVII.
Fourth Cyberpunk Era (4CE) — Campaigns XXII–XXV, named directly in the source; reached via Campaign XXII's 1,000-year jump.

Worth flagging: Campaign V (the Choir Reconstruction, c. 2500 ARV) has a strongly AI-metropolis flavour but is not one of the four — the source material never calls it cyberpunk, and Campaign IX's text explicitly describes itself as laying the foundation for "later cyberpunk eras," implying the lineage starts there, not earlier. Campaign V reads as its own separate, earlier technological swing.

The Choir

Referenced in passing across the Dating entry and the Chain/Veil-as-technology entry without ever getting its own explanation — a distributed will, algorithmic and devotional, trained on the Dominion's own catechism and set loose to complete it in a world of cameras instead of cathedrals. Rising in the city of Veylaris in Campaign V, roughly 2,500 years after the Dominion itself fell, the Choir is proof the Dominion's logic doesn't need Taren personally to keep recurring — it can survive as pure system, encoded into infrastructure rather than carried by a person.

Its central mechanism is the Conductor: the Choir "does what tyrannies do when they lack bodies — it borrows ours," turning wardens into watchers and watchers into conductors until a human face with machine patience speaks the Dominion's old promises in a voice that can pass any biometric test. It falls the same way the Dominion did — not through balance or compromise, but Severance: Taren (a manufactured Choir reconstruction, not the historical man) and Jax Korran descend to the Choir's Core with Iris's backdoor key and shut it down outright. See The Dominion for the pattern it repeats, and Severance for why ending it rather than reforming it is the consistent answer across every age this logic reappears in. Campaign IX's own text explicitly describes itself as laying the foundation for "later cyberpunk eras" — the Choir predates that lineage and reads as its own separate, earlier technological swing rather than the start of it; see the Dating entry below for the fuller distinction.

The Chain / the Veil (as technology)

Beyond its divine-axis meaning, "the Veil" also names the specific reality-shaping magic Taren founds the Dominion on in Campaign II — later broken into Choir engines, Concord law, and eventually resonance across the ages that follow. Its recurring lesson: any system built to guarantee safety or equality by removing difference or choice eventually becomes its own kind of dominion.
